A fridge in a small clinic

Infrastructure, Equipment, IT, HR, Policies & procedures, and Financial resources checks have been conducted in thousands of facilities across 77 countries, scoring each against hundreds of supply chain checks.

Which of those checks actually go with vaccines being in good condition? That is what I set out to answer.

One assessment, three components, and this is about Quality

The WHO Effective Vaccine Management assessment scores a facility against hundreds of checks, each with a code like R0290 called requirements. It also scores a set of outcomes.

  • Outcomes are availability, efficiency, and quality. This analysis is about quality.
  • The applied goal is a shorter instrument, an EVM-Lite. If a short checklist could check only a few things, which ones should they be?

About 45 seconds. Define requirement once, out loud, because every later slide uses the word. Then the division of labour in one breath. Land on EVM-Lite as the destination: that is what the whole talk is walking towards.

Quality is six checks: three during a session, three in the fridge

The composite score QU is the combination of them all.

About 70 seconds, and it earns the time because everything later refers back to this grid. Take the timeline first: a vial is opened at the left mark, doses can be given for 28 days, and anything left after the right mark is waste. Then read the grid as a table. Down the left are the three ways a vaccine goes bad: it expires, it gets too hot, which the vial monitor shows by darkening past its discard point, or it sits opened for too long. Across the top is where the assessor catches it, either watching a real immunisation session or inspecting what is in the fridge. Each failure is checked both ways, which gives six requirements, and the composite QU pools all six. Say that the two in blue are the ones I follow, and that the reason the other four fade out is coming in a couple of slides. Worth flagging now that the left column can only be scored when a session is running, because that fact comes back later to explain a strange result.

The aim here is to explain, not to predict or forecast

Following Shmueli (2010), the question here is which requirements go with quality and explain the variations in qualituy, not how to predict a facility’s score. 1

  • Error metrics such as RMSE will not be reported because a small error would not tell me which requirement matters
  • Only models that hand back a readable number per requirement are allowed to give the answer

About 40 seconds. Say why the distinction bites: a model can be excellent at guessing scores and still tell you nothing you could act on. That rule disqualifies a model later that fits better than the one I use, and I want the panel to see the rule before they see the casualty.

  1. Shmueli, G. (2010). To explain or to predict? Statistical Science, 25(3), 289-310.

How to read every number I show

  • R squared on unseen facilities. Facilities differ in quality. R squared is the share of that difference a fitted model accounts for, from 0 for none to 1 for all. It is always measured on facilities the model was never fitted on.
  • Effect. The change in the quality score, which runs from 0 to 1, for one standard deviation more of a requirement’s score. The causal section later uses a plainer unit: the change from not met to met in full.
  • Conclusive. The model is refitted on 200 random resamples of the facilities. A requirement is conclusive when the middle 95% of its effects across those refits lies on one side of zero, and at least half of the refits keep it in the model at all.
  • Not applicable. Where a requirement does not apply to a facility, the linear models are given the column’s mean. The checks later remove those filled cells and look again.

About 60 seconds. This slide buys back time everywhere else, so do not rush it. The resamples idea in plain speech: shuffle which facilities you happen to look at, refit, and see whether the answer keeps pointing the same way. Say that the mean filling is new in this version of the analysis, and that a later slide removes it to check.

The year column is really a country label

Every one of the 77 countries was assessed in exactly one year. Not one country appears in two different years.

  • So the year tells which group of countries a facility belongs to, and nothing about change over time
  • If the year looks important later, it means countries assessed in some years score better than countries assessed in others
  • I keep the column, but I never read it as a trend, and this comes back twice

About 35 seconds. Small check, large consequence. Flag now that the year turns out to matter for both components, so when it appears the panel already knows how to read it. Without this slide that result looks like a story about improvement over time, which would be wrong.

The best-fitting model is not useful

Eleven model families, each run over twenty repeated splits of the facilities into 75% for fitting and 25% for testing.

  • Random forest has the best raw test score, 0.239 on R0712, but it scores about 0.65 higher on the facilities it was fitted on. It memorises them.
  • XGBoost fits its training facilities perfectly, 1.000, and does worse than a straight line on new ones. Tree models cannot answer an explanatory question here.
  • Among the models that give a readable number per requirement, the elastic net wins all three targets: 0.051 for QU, 0.191 for R0712, 0.180 for R0722.

About 65 seconds. This is the rule from the earlier slide biting. Random forest wins on paper and still loses, and I want that said plainly. The elastic net is worth one sentence: it keeps a small number of requirements and pushes the rest to exactly zero, so what comes out is already a short list.

Knowing the country helps a lot, which is why it is kept out of the predictors

How the ten least useful requirements were chosen

The bottom of a list is harder to define than the top, because the elastic net sets many coefficients to exactly zero and nothing orders them. So the rule has three steps, applied separately to each target.

  1. Set aside the requirements with no room to vary: those met in full by fewer than 5% or more than 95% of the facilities where they apply. A column that never changes cannot earn a low rank.
  2. Among the rest, take the ten kept in the fewest of the 200 resamples. A requirement the model drops almost every time is one it has no use for.
  3. Break ties by the smallest average effect, then by the weakest raw correlation with the target. The correlation is only a tie-breaker; it is not what the model uses.

About 45 seconds. The WHO team asked for this as a credibility check: a method that ranks sensibly at the top should rank sensibly at the bottom too. Explain why step 1 is needed: a requirement everyone meets would otherwise land at the bottom for the wrong reason.

Shuffling the answers finds almost nothing, so the real counts mean something

From association to an adjusted effect, in three steps

Facilities that meet one requirement tend to meet many others, so a simple comparison mixes them all together. Double machine learning compares facilities that are alike on every other column.

Take one requirement, say staff knowing the discard time, and call it the treatment. The quality score is the outcome. Every other column is a control.

  1. Remove from the outcome what the controls can predict. An elastic net predicts the outcome from the controls alone; what it cannot predict is kept. This leftover is a residual.
  2. Remove from the treatment what the controls can predict. A second elastic net does the same for the treatment.
  3. Compare the two residuals. The slope of a straight line through them is the estimate. If the two leftovers still move together, that link cannot come from any of the controls.

The effect is in plain units: the change in the score when a facility goes from not meeting the requirement to meeting it in full. On the two components, 10 points means ten more facilities in a hundred meeting the target.

About 90 seconds. This is the one slide that has to land. Use the discard-time example all the way through. Step 1 and 2 strip out everything the other 171 columns can explain; step 3 asks whether what is left still moves together. Then the unit: not a coefficient per standard deviation any more, but the gap between a facility whose staff know the rule and an otherwise similar facility whose staff do not.

How a result counts, and why country is held fixed

  • Predictions never see the facility’s own country. Whole countries are split into five groups; each group’s residuals come from models fitted on the other four. The split is repeated five times with different groupings and the median is reported.
  • q-value. About 170 columns are tested per target, so some look important by luck. A q-value is a p-value corrected for that. Conclusive means a q-value below 0.05, using standard errors that let facilities in the same country move together.
  • Thin evidence. A column that applies to fewer than a quarter of facilities, or that over 95% of facilities answer the same way, is estimated but not flagged: too few facilities sit on one side of it.
  • Country, two ways. Leaving country out compares facilities across all 77 countries, so differences between countries, in policies, supply systems and assessment teams, can masquerade as requirement effects. Centring every column within its country compares only facilities in the same country, which is the fairer test and the part a country programme can act on. The scan over all 172 columns uses the first to find candidates; the verdict uses the second.

About 80 seconds. Three definitions and one decision. On country: the within-country version is stricter because it throws away all between-country differences, good and bad, and asks only whether a facility that meets the requirement does better than its neighbours in the same country. That is why it is the one the conclusions rest on, and why effects shrink when it is applied.

What the evidence supports

  • Out of 170 requirements across 77 countries, quality at the last mile comes down to a handful, and what staff know is what matters: the discard time of multi-dose vials, the principles of vaccine storage, and the types of opened-vial wastage.
  • Those three pass every test in the analysis: conclusive after adjustment, about half their effect intact within countries, beyond the reach of any measured stand-in for a hidden factor, unmoved when their neighbours leave the controls, and each with a direct route to an opened vial.
  • The requirements that only mark a well-run facility, fire extinguishers, salaries, waste bins, drop out once facilities are compared within their own country.
  • The composite score is a weak target with a hard ceiling. Its one conclusive effect, cold storage capacity, is a measurement artefact: better equipped facilities sit the harder test.

About 70 seconds. Four sentences, slowly. The first is the headline. The second is why it can be believed. The third is what the causal section removed. The fourth is the honest reading of the composite.

What the evidence does not support, and what comes next

  • The screening effects are associations, and the causal effects are causal only if nothing unmeasured drives both a requirement and quality. Every check narrows that doubt; none removes it.
  • Half of each knowledge effect comes from differences between countries. The within-country half is the part a facility can act on.
  • The subcategory results are provisional until the score construction is confirmed with WHO.
  • Next: confirm the subcategory scores; write the named requirements down before the next round of assessments arrives and test them on countries that played no part in choosing them; choose the controls for each requirement from the structure of the EVM framework.

Backup: choosing the model

  • Eleven families over the same twenty splits: ordinary least squares, ridge, LASSO, elastic net, fractional logit, a gamma model on the shortfall from full marks, boosted trees, bagged trees, randomised-split trees, XGBoost and the mean of the training data as a floor.
  • Plain least squares is the weakest linear model on unseen facilities for every target, so the penalties earn their place.
  • The elastic net and the LASSO tie on every target; the elastic net is reported because it handles groups of near-identical requirements more gracefully.

For questions on model choice. One line on AIC if pushed: a criterion computed inside the sample rewards exactly the hard fitting that repeated splits exist to discount.

Backup: how the values that do not exist are handled

  • Some requirements do not apply to a facility, and a linear model still needs a number in that cell.
  • Two options were compared: fill with the column’s mean, or fill and add a marker column saying the value was filled.
  • The markers fit slightly better and double the number of columns, and a marker coefficient is not something a manager can act on, so the simple fill is kept.
  • Two checks limit the cost: the tree families that need no filling name the same requirements, and refitting on fully scored facilities keeps every leading direction.

For questions on missing data. Be straight: markers win narrowly on fit and lose on usefulness.

Backup: quality as four ordered bands

  • The composite score was cut at 0.6, 0.8 and 0.95, and 58% of facilities land in the top band.
  • Always naming the biggest band beats the ordinal model on plain accuracy, 0.582 against 0.496, and the model’s agreement with the truth beyond chance is 0.092, weak by any usual standard.
  • The top twenty requirements change under banding while no measure of fit improves, so the analysis keeps the continuous score.
